If we are serious about learning the Balinese calendar, which is based on pawukon and also sasi(h) , we have no choice but to read the Lontar Bagawan Garga manuscript which is the reference of the ancestors of Bali and Ancient Java in formulating the determination of days in agriculture and also in various rituals.

Lontar Bagawan Garga is a summary of the essence and theology behind the days and wuku , also full of arguments about the harmony of nature that can be applied in everyday life and at the same time can be a deep philosophical reference if listened to seriously.

The next step, after reading the Bagawan Garga Lontar, is to complete it by reading the Aji Swamandala Lontar, which describes the determination of good and bad days ( padewasan ), a teaching from Bhatara Surya Candra. It outlines the tawur ( pecaruan ) ritual and various calendar formulas, which are mentioned in this lontar as guidelines for rituals in Besakih and also as a reference for all regions that follow the Besakih ritual calendar.

Don’t worry, the Bagawan Garga Lontar was transcribed and translated by the Bali Cultural Documentation Center in 1988, and photocopies are available at the Bali Documentation Center. Likewise, a transcript of the Aji Swamandala Lontar is available. While neither manuscript is fully discussed, these two publications or efforts have paved the way for readers attempting to enter the realm of “Balinese wariga knowledge.”

If you want to be specific about the wuku and Hindu ceremonies in Bali, further from the two lontars (Lontar Bagawan Garga and Lontar Aji Swamandala) it seems to be passed down and explained quite systematically into a guideline for the days or calendar of ceremonies, namely in the Lontar Sunarigama which provides a reference to various ceremonies throughout the 210-day cycle, from the Holy Day of Saraswati, various Anggarkasih and Tumpek, then Galungan and Kuningan, until returning to Pagerwesi, back again to Saraswasti. The 210-day cycle in the Sundarigama lontar manuscript is detailed.

By reading the 3 manuscripts in detail, carefully, we are invited to understand the concept and relationship of cosmology, astrology and its relationship to various Balinese rituals.

The Balinese calendar formulated in the Lontar Bagawan Garga and Lontar Aji Swamandala is the reference for writing various other lontars, as well as various other rituals, and various calculations of good and bad for other specific days, which can be found in several such as: Lontar Wariga Gemet, Lontar Wariga Krimping, Wariga Parerasian, Wariga Palalawangan, Purwaka Wariga, and others.

The teachings can be quite broad, ranging from calculating the days for planting beans, taking cattle, piglets , medicine, war, and the issue of auspicious days for cremation and other major ceremonies, to choosing a day to enter the supernatural realm. The Lontar Pengayam-ayam (Chicken Lontar) also discusses calculating the color of a chicken and matching it to the “color of the day” and the “cardinal directions.”

A frequently asked question among Balinese people: Where do the Wariga teachings come from? Who founded them?

Even though these lontars do not clearly explain who formulated them, the name or title of the Bagawan Garga Lontar clearly gives the name of the source of the teachings: Hyang Bagawan Garga.

Who is Bagawan Garga?

Bagawan Garga, or Ṛṣi Garga, or Garga Acarya, or Garga Muni, was a renowned figure in ancient astrology. His teachings spread to almost every ethnic group in Asia. His name is recorded in the Vedas and Puranas.

In the Puranika scriptures he is mentioned as the son of Ṛṣi Bharadvāja. Besides the name Ṛṣi Garga who is recorded as the formulator or expert in astrology and astronomy, as well as the calendar system, the names of other famous Ṛṣis who are experts in astrology are mentioned, such as: Ṛṣi Brahma, Ṛṣi Surya, Ṛṣi Soma, Ṛṣi Vasiṣṭhta, Ṛṣi Pulastya, Ṛṣi Romaka, Ṛṣi Arya.

In Bali itself, the name Ṛṣi/Bagawan Garga is the most famous name and his teachings are mentioned in local teachings when someone learns the teachings of Wariga and Pelintangan, both through oral traditions in calculating tika and titimangsas , as well as in traditional studies through reading lontars passed down from our parents and our great-grandparents.

In the Santi Parva of the Mahabharata, Bagawan Garga is said to have been the “guardian of the saṃvatsara year.” He is also mentioned alongside other great sages: “Then Sukra, in that vast abode of the Lord, was the leader of prayer. The Valakhilyas were his counselors, and the Saraswatas were his friends. The great and renowned Ṛṣi Garga was the reckoner and astrologer of the days.”

In The Brhat-Samhita,’ or Complete System or Natural Astrology of Varahamihira , H. Kern, states that the 1st Century BC South Indian calendar system writer Bala Bhadra who wrote the Horā Ratnaṁ was heavily influenced by Ṛṣi Garga.

Famous ancient books such as Taittirīya-saṃhitā, Pāraskara-gṛhya-sūtra, Varāha-mihira’s Bṛhat-saṃhitā, Bhāgavata-purāṇa mention the saṃvatsara system which was “guarded” by Ṛṣi Garga.

Various ancient sources mention that he was the author of the Garga Horā and Garga Samhita, both of which and the various Gargya-jyotisa legacies are jyotiṣas or ancient treatises on astrology and clairvoyance .

These two texts, Garga Horā and Garga Samhita, appear, as far as I can see, to be references in various Wariga palm-leaf manuscripts that serve as our reference for various forms of art, rituals, and agricultural and other life practices in Bali. Of course, these have been adapted to local names and terminology, local seasonal and astrological cues, and the cultural context of our traditionally inherited agricultural and religious life.

If anyone is wondering why the wariga or Balinese calendar regulates or deals so much with rituals?

It’s also worth hearing this information, that according to several sources, a figure named Ṛṣi Garga is said to be the heir to the Pasupata teachings passed down by Venerable Siva-Srikantha. Venerable Srikantha was a great spiritual teacher whose teachings spread throughout Asia, including Java and Bali. In the Pihgalamata manuscript, a Tantric manuscript inherited from Nepal written in 1174, he is referred to as Bhagavat Srikanthanatha. Lakulisa was likely a disciple of Bhagvat Srikanthanatha. Along with his other disciples, Kusika, Mitra, and Kaurusya, Garga likely passed down the Pasupata teachings, internalized through the calendar he inherited. Lakulisa and his four disciples (Kusika, Garga, Mitra, and Kaurusya) are mentioned in the Candragupta II inscription of the Gupta dynasty. In Bali, the Pasupata teachings seem to be linked to reports in various palm-leaf manuscripts about the arrival of Hyang Pasupati to Bali.

In the Old Javanese and Old Sundanese literary traditions, the teachers who passed down the Pasupata teachings are also mentioned: Kusika, Mitra, Kaurusya, and Garga. Is Ṛṣi Garga the heir to the Pasupata teachings lineage passed down by the Venerable Siva-Srikantha who passed down the Bagawan Garga Lontar? Or do the similarity of names/titles refer to the same expertise? If Ṛṣi Garga is the heir to the Pasupata teachings lineage referred to by the Bagawan Garga Lontar, it is not surprising that its formulation explains many rituals and ceremonies that are closely related to the Pasupata teachings.

If I may suggest, if you want to seriously study the Balinese calendar, it would be best if we first bow down respectfully to the great wariga teachers whom we have ignored and forgotten all this time: First of all, of course, to Hyang Bagawan Garga, then bow down silently to Ṛṣi Brahma, Ṛṣi Surya, Ṛṣi Soma, Ṛṣi Vasiṣṭhta, Ṛṣi Pulastya, Ṛṣi Romaka, Ṛṣi Arya, and also to our ancestral dang-hyangs who have passed down this great tradition to us.

I was intrigued to see entries for titles “Bhagawan Garga” & “Rsi Garga” in the catalog of palmleaf manuscripts at the Gedong Kritya government manuscript library in Singaraja, Bali. Could they be about Sastra Wariga (ज्योतिषं शास्त्रम्)?

Below was the catalog entry: Katalog lontar.jpg Now, I’ve learned that the well known Balinese Vidwan, Sugi Lanus, has written an interesting article here about the same manuscript. Check out its English translation pasted below.

To my pleasant surprise, it is indeed about Jyotisham. The Jyotisham is supposedly aligned with the calendar used in Bali today, ie. It uses both Pawukon पावुकोन् (unique to Bali & Java) & Sasi शशी (chandramana). And that it has already been translated.

FYI, the word Pawukon is derived from the word wuku वुकु, which literally means the same as the word पर्व in Sanskrit & 節 in Chinese, referring to the joints of a bamboo or the section between joints. This wikipedia article describes this 210-day Pawukon calendar with its complex & intriguing grouping of days.
